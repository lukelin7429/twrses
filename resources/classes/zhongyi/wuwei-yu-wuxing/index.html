The Five Flavors, and Why Too Much of a Good Thing Backfires

Unit 41: The Five Flavors, and Why Too Much of a Good Thing Backfires

Chinese medicine matches five basic flavors to the five elements, following the same logic used for organs, emotions, and colors earlier in this unit: sour to wood and the liver, bitter to fire and the heart, sweet to earth and the spleen, pungent to metal and the lung, and salty to water and the kidney. In small, ordinary amounts, each flavor is traditionally said to support its matched organ — a little sourness aids the liver, a little saltiness supports the kidney.

中醫把五種基本味道對應到五行，遵循著這個單元稍早用在臟腑、情緒、顏色上的同一套邏輯：酸對應木與肝，苦對應火與心，甘對應土與脾，辛對應金與肺，鹹對應水與腎。在少量、一般的份量下，每種味道傳統上被認為能支持它對應的臟腑——一點點酸味有助於肝，一點點鹹味支持腎。

The twist is that the same flavor, taken to excess, is said to injure the very organ it normally supports, following the same "too much of a good thing" logic covered elsewhere in this course. Excessive salty food is traditionally linked to strain on the kidney and, by extension, on blood vessels — a claim modern nutrition also recognizes in its own vocabulary, linking high sodium intake to blood pressure. Excessive sweet food is linked to strain on the spleen's transforming function, echoing modern concerns about sugar and metabolism.

有趣的轉折是，同一種味道吃過量，被認為會傷害它平常支持的那個臟腑，遵循著這門課別處也提過的同一套「過猶不及」的邏輯。過鹹的食物傳統上被連結到腎、進而連結到血管的負擔——這個主張，現代營養學也用它自己的詞彙認可，把高鈉攝取連結到血壓。過甜的食物被連結到脾的轉化功能的負擔，呼應著現代對糖分與代謝的關注。

This isn't a coincidence worth dismissing, nor proof that one system copied the other. It's a case of two very different frameworks, working centuries apart with different tools, arriving at a similar practical conclusion: flavor, taken moderately, supports the body; the same flavor, taken in excess, can work against the very function it once supported.

這不是一個值得被輕視的巧合，也不是一套系統抄了另一套的證據。這是兩套非常不同的框架，隔了好幾個世紀、用不同的工具，各自抵達了一個相似的實際結論：適量的味道，支持身體；同一種味道吃過量，卻能反過來對抗它原本支持的那個功能。

Questions · 閱讀理解

According to the passage, which flavor is matched to wood and the liver, and which flavor is matched to water and the kidney?

Sour is matched to wood and the liver; salty is matched to water and the kidney.

According to the passage, what happens when a flavor that normally supports an organ is taken to excess?

It's said to injure the very organ it normally supports, following a "too much of a good thing" logic.

According to the last paragraph, what does the passage say about the similarity between this framework and modern nutrition's concerns about salt and sugar?

It's not a coincidence to dismiss or proof of copying — it's two very different frameworks, working centuries apart, arriving at a similar practical conclusion about moderation.
